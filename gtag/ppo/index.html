Training runs in a background thread, keeps going while you look at the other tabs, and the monke always uses the newest brain. From scratch it reached the 12 m pad on 99% of attempts after about 30 seconds in our two test runs; your machine and your luck will differ.

HOW IT LEARNS

PPO in three steps.

  1. Try things

    Sixteen copies of the monke act with a little random noise added to every hand command, 15 decisions a second.

  2. Score them

    Reward is metres closed to the pad, a small cost per decision, plus 10 for arriving. The pad starts 4 m away and moves to 8 m, then 12 m, as the monke gets reliable. A second network guesses how good each moment was so luck can be told from skill.

  3. Nudge, carefully

    Actions that did better than expected become more likely, but each update is clipped so the policy never jumps far from what just worked. That clip is the “proximal” in PPO.

What is real here

Real: the network, backpropagation, Adam and PPO are written by hand in this repository (gtag/rl/), with no ML library, and they train in your browser on the same Gorilla locomotion port as the course. The net sees 33 numbers and outputs 8: two hand targets and two grips, the same controls you have. Trained brain: the button loads a brain trained ahead of time in Node with this same code; its label says for how long and how it scored. Limits: arriving means getting onto the pad, not stopping on it, and the net often prefers hopping to a tidy walk. Not here: the wall, steps, carry and gap stages, a headset mode, and any code from other projects.

Why your keys and its actions are the same thing

Each key moves a hand's target between a reach pose and a pull pose. The policy outputs the hand target directly. Both go through the same slew limit (5 m/s per axis) and the same physics, so a brain that learns here is doing what you do, only with finer control.
